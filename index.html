What national surveys told us

Services were plentiful. Finding the right one was the challenge.

We did not start by asking what services were missing. We started by reading the national survey evidence, and one finding kept turning up. People generally knew that help existed. Health services, social care, food banks and mental health support were all there in some form, including here in Weymouth and Portland.

What people lacked was confidence. Confidence in which door to try first, that the number they had was still the right one, and that asking would lead somewhere useful rather than another form or another queue.

That single sentence became the design brief for everything that follows.

Removing the friction

One route in, whatever door someone tries first

A person in difficulty rarely arrives with their problem already labelled. Whether the right answer sits in health, social care or the voluntary and community sector, we wanted one route in, not three separate systems each guarding its own front door.

Underneath all three sits something unglamorous: a small, deliberately manageable directory of services. Every entry earns its place and gets checked regularly. A directory that tries to hold everything ends up trusted by no one, because half of it is quietly out of date. A smaller, well kept list beats a comprehensive one that nobody quite believes.

Built and edited in house, with no external supplier or change request in a queue. That speed lets new pages go live within days rather than months, including Living with Pain and Living with Cancer.

For the conversation happening right now

QuickLinks

Built first for reception teams: one short page to pull up in seconds, copy a link from, or read a number off, and hand someone straight to the right service. No scrolling, no searching.

It has since spread beyond the front desk. Some clinicians now keep this page open during appointments. Its value still comes from staying short.

For the moment before someone decides to look

The Local Social

Asks one question, “what’s going on?”, and offers five plain choices: feeling low or stressed, worried about money or food, needing help for family or kids, caring for someone, or not safe at home. No login, no data collected.

Built to travel physically to where people already are: a supermarket noticeboard, the back of a pub toilet door, a college tote bag. Same five choices, wherever someone finds them.

First real world test

Tote bags at the freshers’ fair

On 9 September we handed out tote bags carrying The Local Social QR code at the college freshers’ fair. It was a small launch and a roaring success: of every stand there that day, we were the only tote bag.

A tote bag gets carried for months, on the back of a chair, on a door handle, stuffed with books. Every one of those moments is another chance for someone to find the right door before they have gone looking for it.

Small, and the only one in the room.

How the pieces fit together

Three tools. Three doors. One growing picture.

The PCN website, QuickLinks and The Local Social are three different routes, built for two audiences: staff at a desk or in clinic, and people and patients working things out for themselves. Each one is built to travel to wherever that person already is.

None of them ask for a name or a login. Because nothing is tied to an identity, every search and every tap becomes a small, anonymous signal. On its own, each signal means little. Together, across a whole population, in real time, they start to show a pattern.
